# Cross-validation against SUMO

Generated by `just cross-validate` (`cargo run -p v2xw-interop --bin cross_validate`). Regenerate, do not edit. Seed 20261007; mobility step 100 ms in both simulators; demand for 900 s, 1200 s simulated, measurement window 180–900 s.

## Method

Both simulators drive **the same network, the same trips and the same routes**, and are measured by **the same code**:

1. The network is SUMO's own (`netgenerate` grid; `netconvert` on the cached OpenStreetMap extract of Midtown Manhattan). This engine imports that `net.xml` (`v2xw_world::sumo`), so lanes correspond one to one.
2. Trips are drawn once (exponential headways, uniform origins and destinations over passenger edges, routes of at least 300 m) and routed once by this engine's free-flow Dijkstra.
3. This engine drives them with its native mobility (shipped defaults); SUMO drives them through the `mobility/sumo-traci` plug-in (`crates/v2xw-interop/src/sumo.rs`), which inserts each trip over TraCI with its route, at the same instant, lane and position. SUMO is run twice: its default Krauss model, and IDM.
4. One observer (`crates/v2xw-interop/src/xval.rs`) reads the published states of both: Edie's generalised flow, density and speed over the measurement window and the whole network's lane length; travel time from insertion to arrival, paired by trip; vehicles below 0.1 m/s (SUMO's halting threshold) as the queue; per-edge entries compared with the GEH statistic (acceptance customarily GEH < 5 on 85 % of links, UK DMRB Vol. 12).

## Why the simulators differ

Network, trips, routes, signal programmes and measurement are shared, so what remains is the driving models. Two calibrations of the comparison come first:

- **The same simulator with a different car-following equation moves the results by a few per cent.** SUMO-IDM against SUMO-Krauss (rows above) agrees within about 1–4 % on speed and travel time, with edge-speed correlation 0.95–0.99. Where this engine differs from SUMO by more than that, the difference is not the car-following equation alone: it is the rest of the driver model (lane changing, junction behaviour, insertion).
- **Mean and median travel-time ratios separate a tail from a shift.** Where the paired mean ratio is well above the median ratio, most trips take about as long in both simulators and a minority take much longer in one of them.

The mechanisms that can produce the observed differences, with what each predicts:

- **Lane changing before turns.** SUMO's LC2013 makes strategic lane changes far ahead of a turn; this engine's MOBIL with strategic urgency changes later. A vehicle that reaches the end of its lane in the wrong lane waits for a gap, which slows a minority of trips a great deal and the rest not at all: the tail signature. Not yet isolated by an experiment (next step: rerun with turning trips only, and with SUMO's `--lanechange.duration` and `lcStrategic` varied).
- **Junctions.** Both run netconvert's signal programmes from the same `net.xml`, but yield behaviour differs: SUMO uses its junction model with impatience and look-ahead over internal lanes; this engine uses gap acceptance on the connector lanes the importer builds. The traffic validation report already records this engine's permitted-left critical gap at 5.99 s against the HCM's 4.5 s (`docs/validation/traffic.md`, `calibration/permitted-left-gap`), which lengthens left-turn delays in this engine specifically.
- **Car following.** SUMO's default Krauss has driver imperfection σ = 0.5 (SUMO vType defaults: accel 2.6 m/s², decel 4.5 m/s², τ = 1 s, minGap 2.5 m); its dawdling makes vehicles stop fully more often. Fewer vehicles below 0.1 m/s in this engine at equal or longer travel time is consistent with queues that creep rather than stand.
- **Desired speed.** SUMO draws each vehicle's speed factor from N(1, 0.1) of the lane limit (SUMO `speedFactor` default `norm(1,0.1)`), so about half its vehicles exceed the limit on open links; this engine uses its own per-class desired speeds. This raises SUMO's free-flow link speeds by a few km/h.
- **Insertion.** SUMO inserts at the highest safe speed (`departSpeed=max`); this engine's insertion waits longer for space (the insertion-delay row), though by under a second.
- **Teleports.** SUMO removes a vehicle stuck for 300 s (`--time-to-teleport` default 300 s); none of these runs approaches gridlock, so this does not explain the differences here, but it would at higher demand.
- **Reference point.** Positions are compared at the rear bumper in both (SUMO's front bumper minus vehicle length); this shifts per-edge entry times by under a second and nothing else.

### Why the radio curves differ

- **Channel.** All three studies used WINNER+ B1. This workspace has no calibrated WINNER+ B1 (04-models.md §3.3: the WINNER II D1.1.2 coefficients are not in the cache), only a shaped stand-in (`winner-b1-shaped-todo-calibrate`); the fully cited TR 37.885 highway-LOS channel is shown beside it so the channel's share of the difference is visible. The range at which delivery collapses is set almost entirely by the path-loss slope and breakpoint, which is exactly the uncalibrated part.
- **Geometry.** The harness is a ring with 3 lanes per direction; Veins used 2 lanes per direction on a 5 km straight road, OpenCV2X 3 per direction over 2 km. At equal veh/km the per-lane spacing and so the interferer geometry differ.
- **Error model.** Veins used the link-level FER-vs-Eb/N0 tables of the analytical paper's reference [32]; this engine uses the NIST PER bound for 802.11p and its BLER tables for the sidelink (04-models.md §5).
- **802.11p range.** The largest disagreement in this report: at 300–400 m this engine's 802.11p still delivers most packets on both of its channels, where the Veins study's delivery has collapsed (the Veins curve falls from 0.86 at 200 m to 0.02 at 400 m). On one shared geometry the cut-off distance is set by path loss against receiver sensitivity and the FER table's threshold; WINNER+ B1 is an urban micro-cell model with a steeper loss than either highway-LOS channel this engine can run. Until WINNER+ B1 is calibrated this is a finding about the channel, not yet about the 802.11p implementation (next step: run the sweep with a WINNER+ B1 implementation and the paper's FER table, then compare again).
- **OpenCV2X sub-channelisation.** The paper uses 2 sub-channels of 12 RB in 10 MHz; the harness keeps its 50-PRB pool with 12-PRB sub-channels and sets only MCS (QPSK r0.5) and the −126 dBm RSRP threshold, so its resource pool is larger and its collision rate at 120 veh/km lower.
